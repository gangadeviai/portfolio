Event-Driven Intelligence & Anomaly Detection

Finding Out a Day Late? Event-Driven Anomaly Detection with Kafka and AI Agents

GJ
By Gangadevi JeyarajSenior Agentic AI Engineer · CPO, Enthiram.ai · 15 years in AI
20 November 2026 · 7 min read

The short answer

Put every business event on Kafka, score it in a stream processor with cheap rules and statistical baselines first, and send only the flagged events to an AI agent. The agent gathers context, explains the anomaly and either acts through a gated tool or routes it to a person. Detection happens in seconds, and the language model never sits in the hot path.

Key takeaways

  • Most enterprises detect problems from batch reports, hours after the money or the customer has gone.
  • Use three detection tiers: rules, per-entity statistical baselines, then learned models only where the first two miss.
  • AI agents belong after detection, for triage, explanation and action. They should not score every event.
  • Analyst decisions go back onto a topic, so the system learns from every alert it raises.

A payments team sees the fraud in the morning report. A retailer learns that a pricing job went wrong after a thousand orders shipped at the wrong price. An operations lead hears about a failing integration from a customer. In each case the data that would have warned them existed at the moment it happened. Nobody was listening to it.

I started my career building detection systems for images, then for text, and now for streams of business events. The principle has stayed the same. Detection is only useful if it arrives before the decision it should influence.

Why is batch detection no longer enough?

Instant payment rails give a bank a few seconds to decide. Agentic workflows complete a process in hours that used to take two weeks. When the process itself moves that fast, a nightly job is a history lesson.

There is a second reason in late 2026. Enterprises now run AI agents that take actions on their own. Those agents produce events too: tool calls, approvals, handoffs. If nothing watches that stream, a misbehaving agent can repeat a mistake hundreds of times before a human opens a dashboard.

What does an event-driven design look like?

Every meaningful change becomes an event on Apache Kafka: a payment authorised, a claim updated, a login from a new device, an agent calling a tool. Three decisions at this stage decide whether the rest will work.

  • Key each event by the entity you want to reason about. Customer, account, device or policy. Kafka then keeps that entity's events in order, and your detector sees a coherent history.
  • Register a schema for every topic. Detectors break silently when a field changes type. A schema registry turns that into a deployment error.
  • Use event time, not arrival time. Events arrive late and out of order. Windows based on arrival time produce false spikes.

For systems that cannot publish events, change data capture reads the database log and publishes for them. The legacy application does not need to change.

How should detection be layered?

The mistake I see most often is starting with a complex model. I use three tiers, and I add a tier only when the one before it is not enough.

Tier Method Typical latency Good for
1. Rules Thresholds, allow and deny lists, velocity checks Milliseconds Known bad patterns, regulatory limits
2. Statistical baselines Per-entity moving averages, seasonality, deviation scores Milliseconds to seconds "Unusual for this customer", volume spikes, silent failures
3. Learned models Isolation forests, autoencoders, gradient-boosted classifiers Tens of milliseconds Complex fraud, patterns across many features

Rules are cheap and easy to explain to an auditor. Baselines catch the case rules cannot describe in advance, such as a merchant whose refund rate is normal for the industry and five times its own history. Learned models earn their place when you have labelled outcomes and the first two tiers still miss cases.

All three run inside the stream processor, in Kafka Streams or Flink. They write to an anomalies topic with a score, the features that drove it and the tier that fired.

Where do AI agents fit?

After detection. I do not put a large language model in front of every event. At ten thousand events per second it would be slow and expensive, and its judgement on a single raw event is worse than a baseline's.

An agent is valuable once something has been flagged, because that is when the work becomes messy and contextual. A triage agent consumes the anomalies topic and does what an analyst would do in the first ten minutes:

  1. Enrich. Pull the customer's history, open cases and recent changes from the knowledge layer.
  2. Correlate. Group related anomalies. Forty alerts from one failing upstream system are one incident.
  3. Explain. Write two lines on what happened and why it is unusual, with the evidence attached.
  4. Decide. Choose from a short list of allowed actions: dismiss with a reason, open a ticket, place a temporary hold, notify an owner, or escalate to a person.

In the claims work I led at Enthiram.ai, the fraud check ran before the payout decision for this reason. A fraud signal that arrives after payment is a recovery problem. The same signal before payment is simply an input to the decision.

How do you stop alert fatigue?

An anomaly system that raises five hundred alerts a day will be ignored within a month. Four controls keep volume honest.

  • Deduplicate by entity and window. One alert per customer per hour, updated as evidence grows.
  • Rank by business impact. Score multiplied by exposure. An odd pattern on a dormant account waits.
  • Let the agent close the obvious ones. It records the reason, so the closure can be audited and sampled.
  • Publish every verdict. Analyst decisions go to a feedback topic. Thresholds that produce only false positives are tuned or removed.

That last point is where most programmes stop short. Without the feedback topic, the system never improves and the team stops trusting it.

How do agents act safely on a live stream?

Kafka delivers at least once, so a consumer will sometimes see the same event twice. An agent that acts twice can freeze an account twice, or refund twice. I apply three rules.

  • Every action carries an idempotency key built from the anomaly ID. The receiving system ignores repeats.
  • Reversible actions can be automatic. Irreversible ones need a person. A temporary hold is reversible. Closing an account is not.
  • Failed events go to a dead-letter topic with the error, and are replayed after the fix.

Kafka's retention also gives you a test bench. Before a new detector goes live, I replay last month's events through it and compare its alerts with what analysts decided at the time.

What would I do in your first 30 days?

  • Week 1. Pick one costly, late-detected problem. Map the events that precede it and where they live today.
  • Week 2. Get those events onto Kafka with schemas, using change data capture where needed.
  • Week 3. Ship tier 1 and tier 2 detection. Measure how much earlier you would have known.
  • Week 4. Add the triage agent with read-only tools and a feedback topic. Give it actions only after two weeks of reviewed recommendations.

What changes in 2027?

Agents will become the largest producers of events in many enterprises. Every tool call and handoff is a signal, and agent behaviour will need the same anomaly detection as payments do today: an agent that suddenly calls an export tool fifty times is an incident. I also expect detection to move closer to the action, with the gate that approves a tool call consulting live anomaly scores. I describe that gate in agent observability and continuous evaluation, and the data foundation in unified enterprise data fabric.

Frequently asked questions

Why use Kafka for anomaly detection?

Kafka keeps an ordered, replayable log of events per entity. That gives detectors a consistent history, lets several detectors read the same data independently, and allows new detectors to be tested by replaying past events.

Can a large language model detect anomalies directly on the stream?

It is rarely a good idea. At high event volumes a language model is slow and expensive, and statistical baselines are more accurate on raw events. Use the model after detection, for enrichment, explanation and routing of flagged events.

How do you reduce false alerts in anomaly detection?

Deduplicate by entity and time window, rank by business impact, let an agent close clear false positives with a recorded reason, and feed analyst verdicts back to retune or remove noisy thresholds.

Should I use Kafka Streams or Flink?

Kafka Streams is simpler to operate when the logic is per-entity windows and joins inside Kafka. Flink suits complex event-time processing, large state and multiple sources. Many teams start with Kafka Streams and add Flink when they need it.

How can an AI agent act on anomalies without causing harm?

Give every action an idempotency key, allow automatic execution only for reversible actions such as a temporary hold, require human approval for irreversible ones, and log each decision with its evidence.
