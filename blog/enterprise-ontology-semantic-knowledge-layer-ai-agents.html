Enterprise Ontology & Semantic Knowledge Layer

Why Do Your AI Agents Give Three Answers to One Question? Build an Enterprise Ontology and Semantic Knowledge Layer

GJ
By Gangadevi JeyarajSenior Agentic AI Engineer · CPO, Enthiram.ai · 15 years in AI
20 November 2026 · 7 min read

The short answer

Agents give conflicting answers because your systems disagree on what business terms mean. An enterprise ontology defines those terms once: the entities, their relationships and the rules. A semantic knowledge layer then serves agents through a knowledge graph, a vector index and governed metrics, so every agent reasons over the same meaning.

Key takeaways

  • Most "hallucinations" in enterprise agents are disagreements about meaning, and a better model does not fix them.
  • Start with 20 to 30 real business questions and a core of about 20 entity types. Do not model the whole company.
  • Use the graph for relationships, vectors for documents and governed metrics for numbers. Route each question to the right one.
  • Give agents business-level tools such as get_policy, never raw SQL over hundreds of tables.

Ask three agents in the same company how many active customers there are and you can get three numbers. The sales agent counts accounts in the CRM. The finance agent counts anyone invoiced in the last twelve months. The support agent counts open contracts. Each one is right according to the system it read.

Teams usually call this hallucination and go looking for a better model. A better model will read the same three definitions and produce the same three answers. The fault is in the meaning, and that is where the fix belongs.

What is an enterprise ontology, in plain terms?

An ontology is an agreed description of the things your business deals with and how they relate. A Customer holds one or more Policies. A Claim is made against a Policy. A Claim has an Assessor and may be linked to other Claims through a shared address, bank account or repair shop.

Three terms are often mixed up, so I keep them apart:

Term What it is Example
Ontology The vocabulary and rules "A Claim belongs to exactly one Policy"
Knowledge graph The actual instances, connected Claim 88213 belongs to Policy P-4471
Semantic layer Governed definitions of metrics "Active customer" has one formula

Together they form what I call the semantic knowledge layer. It sits between your curated data and your agents.

Why do enterprise agents need it now?

In a single chatbot, inconsistent meaning produces an occasional wrong answer. In a multi-agent system the damage compounds. One agent's output becomes the next agent's input. If the claims agent and the fraud agent disagree on what "related claim" means, the fraud agent will clear cases it should have flagged.

Text-to-SQL does not rescue you. A model facing six hundred tables with names like CUST_MSTR_V2 will pick a plausible table and write valid SQL against the wrong one. The query runs. The number is wrong. Nobody notices, because nothing failed.

When I designed the knowledge layer for an insurance claims system, this was the part I spent the most time on. The agents were the visible piece. The knowledge layer decided whether they could be trusted.

How do I build the ontology without boiling the ocean?

Ontology projects have a reputation for running two years and delivering a diagram. I avoid that with five habits.

1. Start from questions. I collect 20 to 30 questions the agents must answer, in the words the business uses. "Which open claims share a bank account with a claim we rejected?" Each question names the entities and relationships you need. Anything not needed by a question waits.

2. Keep the core small. A useful first version has around 20 entity types and 30 relationships. In insurance that is Customer, Policy, Coverage, Claim, Payment, Assessor, Provider, Document and a few more.

3. Borrow before you invent. Industry models already exist: ACORD for insurance, FIBO for finance, FHIR for healthcare. Reusing their terms saves months of argument and makes it easier to work with partners later.

4. Resolve identities early. "R. Kumar", "Ravi Kumar" and customer 44021 may be one person. Until records are matched to a single ID, the graph is a set of disconnected facts. This is unglamorous data work and it decides the quality of everything above it.

5. Assign an owner to every term. "Active customer" belongs to a named person in the business. Changes go through review and are versioned, the same way code is.

Which store answers which question?

I do not choose between a knowledge graph and a vector database. Each is good at a different kind of question, and a retrieval router picks per query.

Question type Best source Example
Relationship and multi-hop Knowledge graph (GraphRAG) "Which claims are linked to this repair shop within two steps?"
Meaning in unstructured text Vector plus keyword search "What did the adjuster note about water damage?"
Exact numbers and KPIs Semantic metric layer "Loss ratio for motor policies this quarter"
Policy and definitions Ontology itself "Does this coverage include flood?"

At Pearl Media I ran search across more than five million documents by combining pgvector with Elasticsearch, because neither alone was accurate enough for legal work. The same lesson holds here. Hybrid retrieval beats any single method, and the ontology is what lets the methods agree on what they are retrieving.

How should agents access the knowledge layer?

Through tools that speak the business language. An agent calls get_policy(policy_id) or find_related_claims(claim_id, hops=2). It does not receive a connection string.

This has three effects I care about. The definition lives in one place, so all agents get the same answer. Access control is applied inside the tool, using the identity of the person the agent is working for. And the tools can be exposed over MCP, so any agent framework can use them without custom integration.

How do you know it is working?

Use the questions you started with as a test set. For each one, record the correct answer and check it on every release. Three measures tell you most of what you need:

  • Answer agreement. Do two different agents give the same answer to the same question?
  • Grounding. Can every stated fact be traced to a node, a passage or a metric?
  • Coverage. What share of real user questions map to entities the ontology knows?

When coverage drops, the business has moved and the ontology needs a new term. That is normal. A knowledge layer is maintained like a product.

What would I do in your first 30 days?

  • Week 1. Gather the questions and find the five terms that cause the most disagreement.
  • Week 2. Draft the core ontology with the business owners in the room. Agree on IDs.
  • Week 3. Load one domain into the graph and index its documents. Build four business tools.
  • Week 4. Run the question set through an agent. Fix definitions, not prompts.

What changes in 2027?

Agents are starting to write to the knowledge layer as well as read from it, recording what they learned on a case. That needs validation before anything is stored, or one confused agent will teach the others its mistake. I also expect the ontology to become the contract between agents from different vendors. When your agent hands a task to a partner's agent, both need the same meaning of "approved". I cover that side in A2A and multi-agent coordination, and the data underneath in unified enterprise data fabric.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between an ontology, a knowledge graph and a semantic layer?

An ontology defines the vocabulary: entity types, relationships and rules. A knowledge graph holds the actual connected instances that follow that vocabulary. A semantic layer holds governed definitions of metrics so that each KPI has one formula.

Do I need a graph database to build a semantic knowledge layer?

You need one when questions depend on relationships across several steps, such as fraud rings or supply chains. For document questions a vector and keyword index is enough. Most enterprises end up with both, joined by a shared ontology.

How long does it take to build an enterprise ontology?

A useful first version for one domain takes four to six weeks if you start from 20 to 30 real questions and keep the core to about 20 entity types. Modelling the whole enterprise up front is what makes these projects run for years.

Is GraphRAG better than standard RAG?

GraphRAG is better for relationship and multi-hop questions. Standard vector RAG is better for finding meaning in unstructured text. A router that sends each question to the right method outperforms either one alone.

Who should own the enterprise ontology?

The business owns the definitions and a platform team owns the tooling. Every term has a named business owner, and changes are reviewed and versioned like code.
